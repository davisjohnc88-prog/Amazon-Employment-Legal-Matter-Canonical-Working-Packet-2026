## Caption

> Workplace bullying didn't invent its moves. It inherited them.
>
> For about four centuries, Western institutions have sorted people by a single question: can this person earn their keep?
>
> I traced that question through history in my report, On the Bus or Off the Bus. The same moves kept turning up, first in institutions and then at work.
>
> A eugenics board needed a label, a record, and an audience that would not believe the person being labeled. So does a workplace mob.
>
> This carousel pairs moments from that history with the move each one handed down:
>
> • 1912: a real woman, Emma Wolverton, turned into a pedigree argument → credibility laundering
> • 1917: Army tests where many items rewarded familiarity with American culture rather than reasoning → the invisible standard
> • 1949: a doctor's metaphor that became blame on mothers → the burden shift
> • 1961: a line drawn at IQ 85, later moved, and roughly 14% of people changed category without anyone changing → moving the goalposts
>
> Not the same harm. The same playbook.
>
> Here's the hopeful part. Jennifer Freyd's research found that people who had learned how DARVO works were less fooled by it. A tactic that has a name is harder to run.
>
> And one thing the history asks of all of us: keep your own record. Dated notes, saved messages, and copies of your own requests are not paranoia. They are the file you would otherwise not have.
>
> Which of these have you seen at work, even before you had a word for it?
>
> The full history, the vocabulary, and every source are on my Substack. Link in the first comment.
>
> #WorkplaceBullying #PsychologicalSafety #Neurodiversity #DisabilityHistory
